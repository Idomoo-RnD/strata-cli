## The six control dimensions

Describe an edit through these independent controls instead of calling it merely "fast" or "cinematic":

| Dimension | Low / restrained end | High / intensified end | Main question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shot density | Long takes, fewer angles | Shorter shots, more angle changes | How often must the viewer reorient? |
| Information density | One clear idea per beat | Layered picture, speech, text, and graphics | How much must be decoded at once? |
| Temporal compression | Real time or duration | Ellipsis, montage, jump cuts, time ramps | How much lived time is removed? |
| Spatial continuity | Stable geography and screen direction | Fragmented, subjective, or withheld space | Must the viewer understand where things are? |
| Point-of-view restriction | Observational or omniscient | Strongly attached to one perception | Whose experience organizes the cut? |
| Sonic continuity | Naturalistic, stable perspective | Bridges, counterpoint, rupture, abstraction | Does sound smooth, redirect, or break time? |

These controls can move separately. A one-take scene can feel fast through dialogue, blocking, camera movement, and dense sound. A rapid montage can feel calm if images are simple and music is spacious.

## Primary editing languages

### Continuity or invisible editing

Use when comprehension and immersion should outrank awareness of construction.

- Establish time, place, entrances, exits, and screen direction before compressing them.
- Preserve eyelines, match action across angles, and maintain the 180-degree axis unless reorientation is intentional.
- Cut during motivated movement or attention shifts when a seamless join helps.
- Match the viewer's eye position across frames; fast cutting remains legible when the important subject appears near where the eye already is.
- Use inserts and cutaways for evidence, emphasis, or necessary compression, not random visual variety.

Continuity rules reduce the effort needed to map one shot to the next. Break them when the added effort is the desired experience—disorientation, rupture, alienation, subjectivity—not because coverage is inconvenient.

### Performance-centered editing

Use for drama, comedy, interviews, testimonials, and any scene whose meaning lives in behavior.

- Choose the emotional line across takes before optimizing individual line readings.
- Favor thought, hesitation, breath, listening, and reaction when they change meaning.
- Let shot scale reflect intimacy, power, or realization; do not alternate wide/medium/close mechanically.
- Use L-cuts to keep a speaker's words over the listener when the reception is the event.
- Protect the actor's internal rhythm. A technically clean edit that kills a pause or glance is not an improvement.

### Associative or intellectual montage

Use juxtaposition to create an idea not contained in either shot alone.

- Define the intended inference before selecting images.
- Connect by concept, contrast, cause, shape, color, motion, scale, or sound.
- Allow enough time for the relationship to register; rapid succession is not automatically meaningful.
- Avoid illustrative wallpaper. If narration says "the city woke," a literal city shot repeats; a worker's hands, an empty platform filling, or a changed soundscape may add an argument.
- In factual work, do not imply that unrelated archive depicts the stated event without clear labeling or context.

### Rhythmic or musical editing

Use for music videos, montage, action, dance, sport, promos, and passages organized by sonic form.

- Map phrases, accents, syncopation, breaths, lyrical turns, drops, and silence—not only the beat grid.
- Cut on every beat only when the desired effect is rigid, mechanical, comic, or intentionally overwhelming.
- Build patterns, then omit or delay an expected cut for emphasis.
- Alternate synchronization with counterpoint. A held image over fast music or fast internal motion over a sustained note creates useful tension.
- Prefer movement completion, gaze, lyric meaning, or dramatic consequence over arbitrary beat matching.

### Discontinuity or expressive editing

Use visible construction to communicate fragmentation, instability, urgency, memory, argument, or formal play.

- Jump cuts compress time or create staccato energy; preserve a recognizable anchor so the viewer knows what changed.
- Axis breaks, mismatched action, repeated frames, flash frames, and collision cuts should express a defined state.
- Establish a local rule, repeat it enough to be understood, then vary it.
- Reduce simultaneous complexity elsewhere. Fragmented picture plus dense captions plus chaotic sound often becomes noise rather than intensity.

### Long-take or minimal-cut editing

Use duration to create presence, tension, realism, performance integrity, geography, or moral observation.

- A long take still needs changing composition, blocking, focus, power, or information.
- Hold through the moment at which a conventional edit would offer relief when discomfort is the point.
- Cut when the dramatic unit changes, not merely because the shot has become long.
- Do not preserve a long take as a trophy if the audience loses the story, subject, or essential detail.

### Parallel and cross-cut editing

Use alternating lines of action to imply simultaneity, comparison, convergence, or thematic contrast.

- Give each strand a distinct goal, location, visual signature, or sound motif.
- Re-establish a strand if the absence was long enough to tax memory.
- Shorten alternations as convergence approaches only when urgency should rise.
- Cut away on an unresolved action or question; return with consequence or new information.
- Do not imply simultaneity in factual work unless chronology supports it.

### Multicamera and event editing

Use when a continuous performance or event must remain coherent across angles.

- Establish the rules, participants, stage, court, field, or room before expressive coverage.
- Follow the source of attention: speaker, listener, instrument, ball, result, judge, or audience.
- Cut for meaningful change, not because another camera is available.
- Preserve irreplaceable live timing. Reactions can enrich an event but must not falsely imply chronology in factual programming.
- Build a clean audio or content pass before camera-switch polish when speech or comedy timing dominates.

## Cut and transition vocabulary

| Device | Best use | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Straight/hard cut | Default connection; pace; contrast | Random cutting without a new beat |
| Match on action | Seamless movement across views | Hiding bad geography rather than fixing it |
| Eyeline match / POV | Connect looker, object, and knowledge | Revealing an object too early or falsely |
| Insert | Proof, detail, task, clue, product use | Coverage that interrupts performance |
| Cutaway | Context, reaction, time compression | Wallpaper or false chronology |
| J-cut | Next sound precedes next picture; anticipation or smooth entry | Audio cue spoils a reveal |
| L-cut | Previous sound continues over new picture; reaction or continuity | Flattened scene boundaries or dialogue mud |
| Jump cut | Ellipsis, compression, unease, creator cadence | Accidental-looking discontinuity |
| Graphic/motion match | Link time, place, idea, or energy | Cleverness without story value |
| Smash/collision cut | Shock, punchline, tonal contrast | Repeated shocks lose force |
| Cross-cut | Convergence, suspense, comparison | Confusing timelines or false simultaneity |
| Montage | Compress process, show accumulation or transformation | Generic highlights with no progression |
| Dissolve | Lyrical overlap, time passage, memory | Automatic softness or dated decoration |
| Fade | Major boundary, ending, beginning, loss of consciousness | Punctuation used too often |
| Wipe / designed transition | Chapter, geography, brand or period grammar | Transition pack becomes the subject |
| Whip/occlusion/mask transition | Preserve velocity or hide a motivated spatial join | Motion blur used without matching direction |
| Split screen | Simultaneity, comparison, systems, remote interaction | Competing focal points and unreadable scale |
| Freeze/hold frame | Identification, reflection, irony, graphic handoff | Momentum stops without a reason |
| Speed change | Perceptual emphasis, analysis, subjective time | Artificial excitement or broken motion cadence |

Use the smallest palette that can express the piece. If a transition occurs once, justify it locally. If it recurs, define what it means.

## Sound-picture grammar

Sound frequently determines whether a visual cut feels continuous, anticipatory, comic, or violent.

### Dialogue and voice

- Begin with intelligibility, meaning, and emotional continuity.
- Keep room tone and ambience continuous enough that picture trims do not advertise technical seams.
- Use split edits to preserve natural turn-taking and reveal listening.
- Remove verbal clutter only when meaning, personality, power dynamics, and factual implication remain intact.
- In interviews and news, never build a new sentence or implication the speaker did not make.

### Effects and ambience

- Use sound perspective: a sound's level, detail, and reverberation should reflect narrative distance unless subjectivity says otherwise.
- Let impacts have attack, body, and consequence. Cutting away at the instant of impact without reaction can make action weightless.
- Use pre-laps to pull the audience forward and sound tails to carry emotional consequence.
- Silence is an active contrast. Create it by subtracting expected layers, not merely lowering the master.

### Music

- Decide whether music leads, supports, contradicts, or disappears.
- Map sections and edit points before committing picture to a temp track that may be unavailable later.
- Use stems where possible so dialogue, transitions, and climaxes are shaped rather than volume-ducked crudely.
- Test scenes dry. If story, performance, or action collapses without music, repair the picture and sync-sound construction first.
- Track licensing status from the first music decision.

## Diagnose pacing precisely

Avoid the note "make it faster" until identifying the actual delay.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Better intervention |
|---|---|---|
| Viewer understands early and waits | Redundant lines or images | Enter later, exit earlier, remove repeated proof |
| Viewer cannot follow despite rapid cuts | Information or geography overload | Simplify shots, restore an anchor, reduce layers |
| Scene feels emotionally flat | No reaction, contrast, or escalation | Rebuild performance and consequence, not cut rate |
| Montage feels repetitive | Every shot has same scale, direction, duration, or idea | Vary function and create a progression |
| Action feels weightless | Impacts lack setup, orientation, or aftermath | Add intention, contact, reaction, consequence |
| Dialogue drags | Lines repeat the same beat or pauses lack thought | Trim by beat; preserve meaningful silence |
| Short-form opening feels slow | Promise or value arrives late | Start at the decisive action, result, question, or tension |
| Ending feels abrupt | Action ends but emotional or practical resolution does not | Add result, reaction, choice, or CTA breathing room |

## Review passes

1. **Story-only:** Watch for goals, causality, information order, and change.
2. **Performance/POV:** Track whose experience carries every beat.
3. **Picture rhythm:** Watch muted; mark lost focus, repeated compositions, and arbitrary cuts.
4. **Sound-only:** Listen away from the screen; mark unclear speech, false perspective, flat dynamics, and rough joins.
5. **Dry pass:** Remove or mute temp music to expose weak construction.
6. **Continuity pass:** Check geography, eyelines, action, props, chronology, and lighting only after the story works.
7. **Context pass:** Watch from start to finish at intended size without stopping.

The goal is not invisible editing everywhere. The goal is control over when the audience notices construction and what that notice means.
