# Narrative genre editing

> **Engine anchors.** Most of this is feature-film grammar; it earns its place here as the
> source of *modifiers* - a product reveal cut with thriller information release, a promo with
> trailer escalation, comedy timing for a social spot. Pick one dominant mode from
> [short-form-performance.md](short-form-performance.md) or
> [nonfiction-formats.md](nonfiction-formats.md) and borrow at most two modifiers from here.
> Generated footage for these moods is prompted per [video-generation.md](../video-generation.md).

Use genre as an audience contract, not as a preset. First identify the scene's dramatic job and point of view; then apply the genre conventions that make that job legible and satisfying. A comedy can need a long dramatic hold, an action film can need stillness, and horror can become less frightening when cut rapidly.

## Genre control matrix

| Mode | Audience tracks | Default picture bias | Default sound bias | Rhythm shape |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Character drama | Intention, subtext, change | Performance and reactions | Breath, room, restrained score | Patient with decisive compressions |
| Romance | Mutual perception, distance, reciprocity | Looks, proximity, shared/isolated frames | Intimate detail, musical motifs | Approach, hesitation, connection, separation |
| Comedy | Setup, expectation, violation, reaction | Clean information order | Precise pauses, hard sonic punctuation | Tension and release, not uniform speed |
| Action/adventure | Goal, geography, threat, consequence | Eye trace, movement, cause and effect | Directional cues, impacts, dynamic contrast | Escalating waves with orientation resets |
| Thriller/suspense | Knowledge gaps, deadlines, suspicion | Restricted POV, cross-cutting, details | Pre-laps, clocks, offscreen threat, silence | Tightening intervals with delayed release |
| Horror | Safety, unseen space, dread, shock, aftermath | Negative space, withheld reverse, subjective fracture | Ambience, low-level anomaly, rupture | Often slower than comfort, then violent contrast |
| Mystery/crime/noir | Evidence, inference, unreliable knowledge | Inserts, repetitions, motivated flashback | Motifs and semantic sound bridges | Accumulation, redirection, recontextualization |
| Science fiction/fantasy | Rules, scale, wonder, causal stakes | Orientation before spectacle | World-specific texture and motifs | Exposition relief alternating with awe/action |
| Musical/dance | Phrase, body, space, performance | Full-body continuity plus expressive detail | Song structure and live energy | Phrase-led, syncopated, occasionally sustained |
| Animation/family | Designed performance, clarity, emotional truth | Story-reel precision, readable staging | Dialogue/effects establish performance early | Tuned in animatic, then refined in context |
| Experimental/essay | Association, argument, sensation | Discontinuity, repetition, collision, duration | Counterpoint, voice, silence, abstraction | Rule-based rather than convention-based |

## Character drama

**Viewer promise:** access to behavior, consequence, and emotional change.

### Default grammar

- Build the scene around the character whose internal state changes, not automatically the speaker.
- Select takes for the arc across the entire scene. A brilliant isolated line can damage the character if its energy does not connect to the surrounding behavior.
- Enter after routine has begun and exit once the emotional consequence has registered.
- Let reactions arrive before, during, or after dialogue according to thought—not according to a mechanical shot/reverse-shot pattern.
- Treat silence as action when the audience can read what changes inside it.
- Move closer when knowledge, vulnerability, or pressure changes; earn the close-up.

### Sound and rhythm

- Preserve breath, room tone, cloth, handling, and other intimate details when they keep the viewer present.
- Use L-cuts for listening and emotional carryover; use J-cuts when the next situation should intrude on the current one.
- Score the larger emotional structure, not every line. Test whether a scene remains legible and moving without music.
- Contrast compressed passages with moments allowed to play in duration.

### Failure modes

- Cutting merely to whoever speaks
- Tightening out uncertainty that contains the subtext
- Using wall-to-wall score to declare the emotion
- Choosing continuity-perfect takes that flatten performance
- Mistaking seriousness for slowness or visual monotony

## Romance and intimacy

**Viewer promise:** a legible change in how people see, approach, trust, avoid, or lose one another.

### Default grammar

- Organize eyelines and reaction timing around reciprocity: who looks, who notices, who misses the look.
- Use shared frames to express connection or confinement; isolated coverage to express distance, projection, or asymmetry.
- Let touch, breath, interrupted speech, and spatial distance carry beats that dialogue cannot.
- Delay a reverse shot when longing or uncertainty should grow; reveal it early when mutual recognition is the event.
- Match cuts, repeated blocking, and recurring locations can show relationship change across time.

### Sound and rhythm

- Use sonic privacy: reduce environmental detail or narrow the sound field when the world falls away.
- Carry a voice or motif across time when memory colors the present.
- Preserve pre-contact hesitation and post-contact consequence; trimming only to the kiss, confession, or breakup removes the dramatic shape.

### Failure modes

- Coverage that makes both characters equally available when the story depends on uncertainty
- Music that romanticizes a moment the scene should leave morally or emotionally ambiguous
- Montage that shows activities but no relationship progression

## Comedy

**Viewer promise:** clear setup, controlled expectation, surprise, and enough room to experience the joke.

### Default grammar

- Establish the normal pattern before the violation.
- Protect the order of setup, essential information, reveal, and reaction.
- Use the funniest complete performance, not only the funniest line reading.
- Keep the frame wide when simultaneous behavior or physical timing is the joke; cut close when withheld information or a reaction is the joke.
- A reaction can complete, amplify, or undercut a punchline. It can also ruin it if shown too early.
- After a joke lands, allow the minimum useful recovery before the next setup. Cutting during the audience's laugh can bury information.
- For stand-up and multicamera comedy, build a strong audio/radio pass, then choose camera changes and audience reactions that preserve authentic timing.

### Sound and rhythm

- Use hard cuts, silence, abrupt music stops, and offscreen sound as punctuation.
- Do not add cartoonish effects by default. The contrast between serious treatment and absurd content may be funnier.
- Maintain tiny timing differences deliberately; comedy often changes at frame-level trims.

### Failure modes

- Cutting faster because the material is comic
- Showing the reaction or object before the setup completes
- Removing awkwardness when awkwardness is the engine
- Using false audience reactions or sweetening that changes the perceived response
- Overlapping dialogue so aggressively that wordplay becomes inaudible

## Action and adventure

**Viewer promise:** understandable goals and cause-and-effect delivered with danger, speed, scale, and physical consequence.

### Build every action unit

Use this causal chain as a diagnostic, not a mandatory shot list:

1. **Intention:** who wants what now?
2. **Obstacle or threat:** what changes the plan?
3. **Orientation:** where are people, objects, exits, hazards, and objectives?
4. **Attempt:** what action is taken?
5. **Contact or near miss:** what physically occurs?
6. **Reaction:** who perceives the result?
7. **Consequence:** what new problem or advantage follows?

The audience need not see every step every time, but confusion often comes from removing one that carries causality.

### Default grammar

- Establish geography before fragmenting it. Reset with a wide, map, repeated landmark, screen direction, or clear objective when the situation changes.
- Keep critical action near a consistent eye-trace zone across rapid cuts.
- Cut on movement for velocity, but sometimes hold through an entire stunt, strike, or fall to preserve scale and credibility.
- Vary shot size by function: wide for trajectory and difficulty; medium for interaction; close for decision, hazard, contact, or injury.
- Cross-cut only when each line has a goal and their convergence matters.
- Use speed changes for perception or analysis, not as automatic hype.
- Give major impacts aftermath. Without reaction, debris, changed sound, or strategic consequence, action becomes weightless.

### Sound and rhythm

- Make sound directional and causal: cue threat, motion, contact, and environment.
- Preserve dynamic range. If every moment is loud and dense, nothing feels large.
- Use dropouts, ringing, breath, or narrowed perspective to enter subjectivity after a major event.
- Alternate propulsion with orientation and recovery. Shorter shots alone do not create escalation.

### Failure modes

- Cutting before action becomes readable
- Constant close coverage with no geography
- Repeating impacts without changing the tactical situation
- Music and effects masking dialogue or the physical logic of the scene
- Treating every action scene as a trailer montage

## Thriller and suspense

**Viewer promise:** controlled uncertainty, consequence, and anticipation.

### Choose the knowledge design

- **Suspense:** audience knows a threat the character does not; use cross-cutting, offscreen sound, or revealed details to sustain anticipation.
- **Mystery:** audience and character lack an answer; organize clues fairly and manage inference.
- **Surprise:** audience and character learn together; protect the reveal from premature picture or sound cues.
- **Dramatic irony:** audience understands the meaning of behavior differently from the character; reaction selection is critical.

### Default grammar

- Restrict point of view consistently; switch POV only for a clear narrative advantage.
- Show goals, deadlines, resources, and costs early enough that small events carry weight.
- Delay the reverse shot or result when anticipation is the experience.
- Use inserts as clues, not decoration. Control their duration so the audience can register but not over-interpret them.
- Tighten alternation intervals as lines of action converge, then consider a longer hold at the decisive moment.
- Let false resolution create a new vulnerability rather than repeating the same threat.

### Sound and rhythm

- J-cut an approaching sound to make the future invade the present.
- Use recurring mechanical or environmental cues as clocks or threat signatures.
- Subtract expected sound before a reveal; contrast is stronger than adding another layer.
- Do not let temp score reveal the editorial strategy too early.

### Failure modes

- Confusing the viewer about basic facts instead of making them uncertain about outcomes
- Withholding information the viewpoint character plainly knows, solely to trick the audience
- Cross-cutting unrelated events so viewers infer false simultaneity
- Repeating a suspense pattern without escalation or new knowledge

## Horror

**Viewer promise:** vulnerability, dread, violation, shock, or lingering unease within a coherent tonal contract.

### Default grammar

- Decide whether fear comes from anticipation, uncertainty, disgust, helpless observation, subjectivity, or shock.
- Suspense often benefits from shots held slightly beyond comfort. Fast cutting can release pressure by giving the viewer too much information.
- Use negative space and withheld reverses. A character looking offscreen does not always require immediate payoff.
- Control safe and unsafe geography. Repeatedly establish doors, corridors, shadows, or exits so deviations matter.
- For subjective panic, fragment space and continuity after the audience understands what stability looked like.
- Treat the scare as setup, expectation, disruption, and aftermath. The aftermath often determines whether fear lingers.
- Vary scare grammar. If every scare uses the same music rise and smash cut, the audience learns the mechanism.

### Sound and rhythm

- Build dread from ambience anomalies, distance, silence, and ambiguous offscreen sources.
- Do not over-score. A credible room with one impossible sound can be more disturbing than a continuous drone.
- Use low-detail or muffled perspective to restrict information; restore full bandwidth for rupture.
- Protect quiet enough that the next sound has contrast.

### Failure modes

- Cutting away from disturbing duration too early
- Explaining the threat so thoroughly that imagination has no work
- Confusing darkness with unreadability
- Using flash frames, strobing, or high-intensity sound without accessibility and safety review
- Equating gore coverage with fear when consequence and character attachment are absent

## Mystery, crime, and noir

**Viewer promise:** evidence and human motive combine into inference, revelation, or moral ambiguity.

- Separate fact, testimony, assumption, and memory in the edit language.
- Use recurring details to recontextualize earlier scenes; a repetition should add knowledge, not fill time.
- When flashbacks represent uncertain testimony, signal the epistemic status through framing, texture, sound, or later correction.
- Let viewers see enough evidence to participate, unless the intended mode is character study rather than puzzle.
- In procedural scenes, compress routine but preserve the step that changes the case.
- Use noir ellipses, voice-over, or associative montage to create subjectivity without making causality arbitrary.

Avoid a reveal that depends on hiding ordinary coverage the audience reasonably expected to see. Fair misdirection emphasizes an alternative interpretation; unfair misdirection removes the evidence.

## Science fiction and fantasy

**Viewer promise:** a coherent unfamiliar world whose rules matter emotionally.

- Introduce one new system, space, creature, or rule at a time when possible.
- Orient before spectacle: show the objective and scale so visual effects have meaning.
- Use inserts, displays, maps, or exposition only when they change a decision. Interface graphics should clarify story points at viewing speed, not simulate complexity.
- Repeat visual and sonic motifs for technology, magic, factions, locations, and rules.
- In battles, apply action causality; in wonder sequences, extend duration so scale and discovery register.
- When reality becomes unstable, establish the stable grammar first and change it in trackable stages.

Avoid compressing world-building so aggressively that later stakes depend on rules the audience never absorbed.

## Musical and dance narrative

**Viewer promise:** performance, story, and musical form feel causally integrated.

- Map intro, verse, pre-chorus, chorus, bridge, instrumental break, reprise, and ending, plus lyrical turns and choreographic phrases.
- Decide whether the sequence prioritizes live performance integrity, emotional POV, spectacle, or narrative progression.
- Preserve enough full-body and wide coverage to read choreography and enough sustained takes to believe performance.
- Cut detail for accents, character thought, contact, or transition—not simply because a beat occurs.
- Use phrase boundaries, breath, movement completion, syncopation, and lyrical meaning as edit points.
- Build visual repetitions that evolve with musical repetitions.
- When music stops, make the new silence narratively consequential.

Avoid beat-locking every cut. It can make even expressive music feel mechanical and remove surprise.

## Animation and family storytelling

**Viewer promise:** designed images feel like living performance and remain readable at the intended audience's processing level.

### Story-reel method

1. Assemble dialogue or vocal performance.
2. Time storyboard poses and changes to thought, action, and speech.
3. Add essential effects and ambience that define physical behavior.
4. Add music after the scene's performance and story rhythm work.
5. Screen the sequence in context and revise before expensive animation proceeds.

- In animation, editorial helps create performance before final footage exists. A pose held too briefly or too long can kill a joke, emotion, or action.
- Treat new animated shots as production cost. Prove their story function in boards, animatic, previs, or layout.
- Maintain clear silhouettes, staging, and focal hierarchy; do not solve every timing issue by adding camera motion.
- For children and family work, preserve emotional complexity while reducing avoidable simultaneity and unreadable text.
- Replace boards with animation progressively without losing the approved timing, then allow new performance detail to justify refinements.

## Experimental, essay, and poetic film

**Viewer promise:** form itself produces association, argument, sensation, or reflection.

- Define the governing rule: repetition, duration, collision, voice/image contradiction, archive taxonomy, loop, accumulation, or spatial pattern.
- Give the audience at least one anchor: a voice, recurring image, chronology, location, question, or musical structure.
- Let discontinuity be legible as a choice. Random variation has less force than a rule that changes under pressure.
- Use silence, black, leader, text, or empty duration as material when it changes attention.
- Distinguish ambiguity from incomprehensibility. The viewer may not know the answer but should sense what kind of question the film asks.
- In essay and archive work, retain factual labeling and source integrity even when associations are poetic.

## Hybrid selection examples

| Brief | Dominant mode | Modifier | Resulting edit strategy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Romantic action scene | Action causality | Romance POV | Keep geography clear, but land decisive beats on mutual looks and risk |
| Horror comedy | Comedy information order | Horror duration/sound | Hold credible dread, then use precise rupture or reaction as punchline |
| Sci-fi mystery | Mystery evidence | World-rule clarity | Reveal unfamiliar systems only as clues become actionable |
| Sports drama | Character drama | Action/sports legibility | Build plays around the protagonist's choices and consequences, not generic highlights |
| Animated musical | Animation story-reel | Musical phrase | Prove performance and story timing in the animatic before final animation |

When conventions conflict, follow the scene's viewer promise. State the tradeoff rather than pretending one solution satisfies every genre equally.
